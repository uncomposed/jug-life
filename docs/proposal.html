Jug Twin

A pocket simulator of one gallon of gluten-free beer fermenting in a glass jug. You watch it the way a brewer would, through a few cheap sensors. Then you pull back the curtain and watch the yeast, sugar, alcohol and heat that are really driving those readings.

The setup it models: a 1-gallon glass jug, a drilled rubber stopper and an S-shaped airlock. No sensors yet, which turns out to be useful (see "Pick sensors before you buy them").

Mock-up, not the real app. Every number here is illustrative. The sensor tags use the standard instrument codes (TI = temperature indicator, DI = density indicator, FQ = flow counter), always with a plain label next to them. The dashed circle marks a reading you take by hand.

The idea in one paragraph

One jug, two views, one clock. The sensor view shows only what a real brewer could see: a thermometer strip, airlock bubbles, the odd hydrometer reading, all with realistic noise and lag. Behind the curtain shows the true state: yeast multiplying then settling, sugar falling, alcohol rising, CO₂ leaving, the jug warming itself a little. A time scrubber drives both views, so you can drag back to "why did the bubbling stop on day 3?" and see the answer underneath. You can also act on it: pitch, warm it, cool it, rouse the yeast, add nutrient, swap to a blow-off tube. Some actions help and some make things worse, just like the real thing.

What already exists

Three research agents surveyed the field. The short version: lots of tools log fermentation, a few industrial models simulate it, and nothing we found simulates a homebrew batch you can play with. That gap is the project.

What to steal

  1. Brewfather's "expected curve with milestones" overlay.
  2. The iSpindel trick of simulating the raw signal, then converting it, so you see the same error a real device would make.
  3. A cut-down published kinetic model (details below).
  4. BeerJSON, the open recipe format, for loading a recipe.
  5. A bubble rate derived from simulated CO₂, with the noise and leaks that sank Plaato left in on purpose.

Pitfalls to avoid

  1. Treating bubbles as a gravity reading. They are a vibe, not a measurement, and the sim should teach exactly that.
  2. Anything cloud-dependent. Keep it a single local page.
  3. Pretending to accuracy. Published model constants come from industrial lager, not sorghum in a gallon jug.
  4. Building the engineering before the fun. A beautiful, wrong-ish sim beats a correct one nobody opens.

What's going on under the hood

A small model with four moving parts, based on the published beer models (Gee and Ramirez 1988, de Andrés-Toro 1998) but cut down to what a gallon jug needs.

  1. Yeast wakes up after a lag, multiplies while there's sugar and nutrient, slows as alcohol builds, then drops out and settles.
  2. Sugar gets eaten at a rate set by how much yeast there is and how warm it is.
  3. Alcohol and CO₂ come out in fixed proportions: each gram of sugar makes about half a gram of each.
  4. Heat from fermentation warms the jug, while the jug leaks heat to the room. In a gallon, the room mostly wins.
growth dX/dt = μmax · S/(Ks+S) · (1 − E/Emax) · f(T) · lag(t) · X − kd·X sugar dS/dt = −q · X · S/(Ks+S) · (1 − E/Emax) · f(T) alcohol dE/dt = 0.47 · (−dS/dt) CO₂ ≈ 0.46 · (−dS/dt) heat C·dT/dt = ΔH · (−dS/dt) − (T − T_room)/R

X yeast, S sugar, E ethanol, T temperature. f(T) is a temperature curve for ale yeast (sluggish below about 15 °C, off-flavors above about 24 °C).

A gallon by the numbers

Starting gravity 1.050, sorghum extract, about 70% of the sugar fermentable:

  • About 490 g of sugar in the jug; about 345 g of it gets fermented.
  • About 160 g of alcohol, roughly 4.5–5% ABV.
  • About 165 g of CO₂, around 85 litres of gas. At roughly 2 mL a bubble, that's about 40,000 airlock bubbles over the whole batch. At peak that is roughly one bubble every 3 to 5 seconds. (An earlier draft said a few per second; the built model corrected it.)
  • Only about a degree warmer than the room at peak. A 5-gallon batch can run several degrees hot; one gallon sheds heat too fast.
  • One 11.5 g sachet of dry yeast is about five times what a gallon needs, a good "pitch rate" lesson.
These numbers are estimates. The research agents could not open the papers with the fitted constants, and sources disagree about threefold on the heat fermentation gives off. The sim will mark every tunable number and say where it came from. It is unvalidated until it's compared against a real batch.

Gluten-free mode

This is where the sim gets interesting, because gluten-free brewing fails in specific, simulatable ways.

  • Gluten-free vs gluten-reduced. In the US, "gluten-free" means under 20 ppm. A sorghum or millet beer is gluten-free by ingredients. A barley beer treated with an enzyme like Clarity-Ferm is only "gluten-reduced", because the tests for it are contested. The recipe picker teaches this.
  • Hungry yeast. Sorghum wort is low in the nitrogen yeast need (FAN), so fermentations start slow and stall. Adding yeast nutrient is a real lever in the sim.
  • Higher finish. Sorghum extract ferments less completely, so the beer ends sweeter and thinner (finishing gravity around 1.010–1.020).
  • The sorghum tang. A known sour note; the tasting card at the end mentions it.
  • All-grain later. Millet, buckwheat and rice need added enzymes and hotter mash temperatures. That's a stretch goal; extract first.

Things that can go wrong (the fun part)

Blow-off

A full gallon jug has almost no headspace. Foam climbs, clogs the airlock, pressure builds, the stopper pops. Fix: blow-off tube, or fill less.

Suck-back

The room cools overnight, the gas in the jug shrinks and pulls airlock liquid into the beer. Fix: steady temperature, sanitizer in the airlock.

Stuck ferment

Too cold or too little nutrient and the yeast quit early. Fix: warm it, rouse it, add nutrient.

Hot ferment

Above about 24 °C you get harsh fusel and fruity esters. The sim tracks a "flavor debt".

Infection

Skipping sanitation rolls dice. Wild yeast or bacteria show up days later as gravity creeping below where it should stop.

Leaky stopper

No bubbles, but fermentation is fine. The classic beginner panic, and the reason the curtain view exists.

Pick sensors before you buy them

His jug has no sensors yet, which is a feature. The sim lets him add virtual ones and see what each would and wouldn't tell him:

  • Stick-on thermometer strip (a few dollars): reads the glass, not the beer, in 1–2 °C steps.
  • Digital probe: about ±0.5 °C, a little lag.
  • Bubble counter: activity, not progress. Lies when the stopper leaks.
  • Hydrometer or refractometer sample: the real answer, but by hand, and you lose a little beer each time. The refractometer needs a correction once alcohol is present.
  • Floating hydrometer (Tilt, iSpindel): great data, won't fit the neck. The sim can say so.

Build plan

1

A jug you can watch (one weekend)

The core model, the schematic with sensor and curtain views, the time scrubber, one recipe (sorghum extract, US-05-style ale yeast). Single web page, runs offline.

2

A jug you can mess up

Actions (temperature, rouse, nutrient, blow-off tube) and the failure events above. A "what changed?" note whenever the phase shifts.

3

A jug that matches his jug

He brews a real batch with a thermometer strip and a few hydrometer readings, logs them, and the sim overlays them on its prediction. That's the first real validation, and the most interesting result either way.

4

Stretch goals

All-grain gluten-free mashing with enzymes, a mass-balance flow chart (sugar splitting into alcohol, CO₂ and heat), bottle conditioning and carbonation, a "brew day" story mode.

How we'd know this was the wrong call

  1. If his real batch's gravity curve lands far outside the sim's range even after tuning, the cut-down model is too simple and we need the fuller published one.
  2. If he opens it once and never again, the fun loop (actions and events) matters more than the physics, so we shift effort there.
  3. If he never looks behind the curtain, the two-view idea isn't landing and the schematic needs rethinking.

Questions for your friend

  1. Extract (sorghum syrup) or all-grain (millet, buckwheat)? This decides whether phase 1 includes mashing.
  2. Which yeast is he planning on?
  3. Where will the jug live? A closet with a steady temperature or a kitchen that swings overnight changes which failure he's most likely to hit.
  4. Is he willing to buy a $5 thermometer strip and take three hydrometer readings? That's all phase 3 needs.
